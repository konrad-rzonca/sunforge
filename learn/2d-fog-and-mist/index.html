Fog and ground mist in a Unity 2D scene

Unity's fog setting does not reach sprites, so in a 2D URP scene you make fog out of sprites, particles or a full-screen pass. This article is about fog as weather. Fog of war, which hides the parts of a map the player has not explored, is a different thing and is not covered here.

Why Unity's fog does nothing in a 2D scene

The fog setting is in the Lighting window (Window > Rendering > Lighting), on the Environment tab under Other Settings. It has a color and a mode, and it gets denser with distance from the camera.

Turn it on in a scene of sprites and nothing changes. URP's sprite shaders, Sprite-Lit-Default and Sprite-Unlit-Default, do not apply it, and neither does a Sprite Lit or Sprite Unlit Shader Graph. A shader that did apply it would not help much: the sprites of a 2D scene are all about as far from the camera, so the fog would tint them all by the same amount.

Unity 2D volumetric fog

Unity's volumetric fog belongs to HDRP, and URP has none. What reads as volume in a 2D game is layers: a few sheets of fog at different depths that move at different speeds.

Four ways to make a 2D fog effect

A scrolling noise sprite

This suits fog over a wide area.

  1. Import a soft noise texture that tiles, and set its Wrap Mode to Repeat.
  2. Choose Assets > Create > Shader Graph > URP > Sprite Unlit Shader Graph.
  3. In the graph, multiply a Time node by a small speed and connect the result to the Offset of a Tiling And Offset node. Sample the noise with that node's output.
  4. Multiply the sample by your fog color, and fade its alpha toward the top of the sprite so the fog has no hard upper edge.
  5. Make a material from the graph and put it on a Sprite Renderer that covers the area. Set its Sorting Layer and Order in Layer so it draws between the layers you want.

Two such layers that scroll at different speeds look less like one sliding picture.

A particle system of soft sprites

This suits a patch of mist, such as over a pond or in a doorway. Use a Particle System that emits a few large, slow particles with a soft cloud texture at low alpha. Give it a material with URP's Particles Unlit shader and Surface Type set to Transparent. In the Renderer module, Sorting Layer ID and Order in Layer sort it among your sprites.

A full-screen pass

URP's Full Screen Pass Renderer Feature draws a material over the whole screen. Select your renderer asset, click Add Renderer Feature, and give the feature a material made from a Fullscreen Shader Graph. Its Injection Point sets when it draws: Before Rendering Post Processing draws it after transparent objects and before post-processing.

A full-screen pass covers everything drawn before it, so it cannot sit between two layers. For that, the 2D Renderer has Camera Sorting Layer Texture: it saves the picture as it is after a sorting layer you choose, and a fog shader on a later layer can read it.

Tinting far layers toward the fog color

This adds nothing to draw. Set the Color of each parallax layer's Sprite Renderer toward the fog color, a little more on each layer further back. The color multiplies the art, so it can only darken. For pale fog over dark art, blend toward the fog color in a shader, or repaint the layers.

Traps

The fog covers a character from head to foot. A fog layer is a flat sheet. Sorted in front of a character, it hazes the whole figure. Sorted behind, it leaves the whole figure clear. Ground mist should be thick at the feet and thin at the head. In a side view you can get close with a low band in front of the characters that fades out upward. In a top-down or isometric scene the characters stand at many depths, so one band is wrong for most of them. You can cut the mist into strips and sort them among the characters, at the cost of more layers.

Sorting. Unity orders sprites by sorting layer, then by Order in Layer, where lower values draw behind higher ones, then by distance from the camera. Give the fog a sorting layer or an order of its own. If it shares both with other sprites, its place among them depends on that distance.

Overdraw. Each transparent layer is drawn over what is behind it, so three full-screen fog layers fill every pixel three more times. A phone's GPU is the first to slow down from it. URP's Rendering Debugger (Window > Analysis > Rendering Debugger) has an Overdraw view that shows where pixels are drawn over one another. Use a few large particles instead of many small ones, and a small texture: soft noise looks the same at a low resolution.

Fog that ignores the lights. Unlit fog keeps its color whatever the light, so at night it stays gray beside a fire and pale in the dark. To have your Light 2D lights reach it, build the graph as a Sprite Lit Shader Graph, and include the fog's sorting layer in each light's Target Sorting Layers. The light then falls on the fog as it would on a flat wall.

Banding. A soft gradient at low alpha shows steps. Set the fog texture's Compression to None. On the camera, under Rendering, turn on Dithering, which reduces banding on wide gradients and in dark areas.

How Sun Forge 2D Lighting does ground mist

Sun Forge 2D Lighting is a lighting package for URP. Mist is part of its weather, and it drifts over the ground as its cloud shadows do. It works under URP's 2D Renderer and its Universal Renderer, in a top-down or isometric scene seen by a camera that faces the sprites straight on. In a side view its mist does not show, because a side view shows no ground for it to lie on, so a platformer needs one of the ways above.

Its mist is not a layer in front of the sprites or behind them. Each sprite that Sun Forge bakes from a 3D model has a Geometry texture, which holds the height of each texel above the sprite's pivot. So the mist covers each sprite from the feet up.

Every Light Source lights the mist too.

Sun Forge needs Unity 6.3 LTS (6000.3.28f1 or newer) or Unity 6.6.
